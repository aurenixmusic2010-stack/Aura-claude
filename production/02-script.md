## COLD OPEN — 0:00–0:50 · blocks 1–5

> In late twenty twenty-three, the chief executive of a Danish pharmaceutical
> company started getting some very strange phone calls.
>
> They weren't from doctors. They weren't from patients. They were from food
> executives. And by his own account, they were scared.
>
> Here's what scared them. In about three years, one weekly injection had
> switched off the appetite of roughly eleven million Americans.
>
> Watch it spread. County by county, prescription by prescription, across a
> country that spent fifty years learning how to sell people more.
>
> Because the American food business ran on a single assumption nobody ever
> bothered to write down. That you would be hungry tomorrow.

**Visual.** Open on black. One ringing landline, alone in the sound field. Cut to
paper-cutout illustration — a phone receiver on a desk, Copenhagen skyline behind
it in flat navy. Hard cut to the US map.

**The map is the anchor.** It builds under block 3 and lands its payoff at
**0:38**, inside block 4 — prescription density filling in county by county,
2021 → 2026. This is the first big visual payoff of the video and Vox's standard
geographic anchor inside the first 75 seconds. It is paid back by the coverage
map in block 69.

Block 3 carries the count-up: an abstract tally climbing to eleven million, then
holding. Per the no-text rule this is rendered as **stacking cutout figures**,
not numerals — the real figure lands in the burned subtitle.

**Note on the quote.** Jørgensen's actual words to Bloomberg (Feb 8, 2024) were
*"A couple of CEOs from, say, food companies have been calling me. They are
scared about it."* The script paraphrases rather than quotes, which is why block 2
says "by his own account" — that phrasing is load-bearing, keep it.

## ACT ONE — THE BUSINESS OF BEING HUNGRY — 1:00–4:00 · blocks 7–24

> To understand why one drug scared a trillion-dollar industry, you have to go
> back to when American food got very, very cheap.
>
> In the nineteen sixties and seventies, new crop varieties and new farm policy
> did something the food business had waited a century for.
>
> They collapsed the cost of a calorie. And that created a simple math problem
> with an extremely attractive answer.

**The ratchet — blocks 10–13.**

> Say you run a restaurant. Doubling the pasta on a plate costs you maybe fifty
> cents. The customer will pay five dollars more.
>
> Because a bigger plate feels like a better deal. So you make it bigger. Then
> your competitor makes theirs bigger.
>
> Then everybody's is bigger, and nobody can go back, because the customer now
> reads normal as the new size.
>
> That's not a conspiracy. Nobody had to plan it. It's a ratchet. And a ratchet
> only turns one way.

This is the intellectual spine of Act One and it must not be rushed. The
through-line plate does its first growth here — one size per block, ratcheting,
never shrinking back.

**The portion history — blocks 14–17.**

> Portion sizes started growing in the nineteen seventies, climbed sharply
> through the eighties, and then never really came back down.
>
> The McDonald's french fry serving that was the only size on the menu in the
> nineteen fifties eventually became the small.
>
> Coca-Cola's original bottle held six and a half ounces. By the nineties, a gas
> station would happily sell you sixty-four.
>
> Close to eight hundred calories. About a third of what an adult needs in a day.
> In a cup with a handle on it.

**Visual.** Cutaway sequence — hamburger, bagel, soda cup, movie popcorn, each
morphing from 1970s size to current size, held **side by side at the same scale**
so the growth is undeniable. Same-scale is the whole trick; do not let the
compositor re-frame between the two states.

**The chart — blocks 18–20.** ⚠ *Numbers corrected. See fact-check §4.*

> Add that up across an entire population, and you get one of the most important
> charts in modern American health.
>
> Adjusted for waste, the average American took in about two thousand and fifty
> calories a day back in nineteen seventy.
>
> By twenty ten, it was near two thousand five hundred. Same country. Same
> genetics. Twenty-two percent more food, every single day.

**Visual.** Line chart building left to right, 1970 → 2010, calories per capita.
Single accent line, everything else muted. Palette flips to navy for these two
blocks — the only place in Act One that happens, so the chart reads as evidence
rather than illustration. When the line crosses its midpoint, dim the background
and let the shape sit alone for a full beat.

**The bliss point — blocks 21–24.**

> And the food itself was changing. Inside the industry there's a term for the
> exact ratio of sugar, salt and fat.
>
> The ratio that makes a product maximally pleasurable without ever hitting the
> point where your brain says stop. They call it the bliss point.
>
> There's a reason a chip melts the instant it hits your tongue. Food that
> vanishes fast tells your brain very little. So you reach again.
>
> Fifty years of this produced a food environment genuinely excellent at one
> thing. Getting you to eat past the point you needed to.

## ACT TWO — THE SIGNAL — 4:10–7:00 · blocks 26–42

**Mechanism — blocks 26–30. Keep this under 45 seconds.** This is not a science
video and most of the audience already knows the drug.

> Your body already makes a hormone called G L P one. You release it after you
> eat, and it does three things.
>
> It tells your pancreas to put out insulin. It tells your stomach to empty more
> slowly. And it tells your brain: enough.
>
> The problem is it clears out of your system in a couple of minutes. The signal
> fires once, and then it's gone.
>
> Semaglutide, the molecule inside Ozempic and Wegovy, is a version of that
> hormone engineered to stick around for days instead of minutes.
>
> So the enough signal doesn't fire after dinner and fade out. It just stays on.
> All week. Every week.

**Visual.** Flat vector gut-brain axis. Natural GLP-1 as small dots appearing and
vanishing in seconds; then the same dots persisting across a seven-day bar. This
is where the through-line plate **stops growing for the first time** and a marker
line draws across it.

**The food noise — blocks 31–33.**

> Users describe the effect in almost identical language, over and over again.
> They call it the food noise going quiet.
>
> That background hum of wanting something, the one that used to run all day in
> the back of your head, simply stops.
>
> And that's the exact moment a medical story turns into an economic one. Because
> appetite was somebody's business model.

Block 33 is the pivot of the whole video. Everything before it is setup.

**The economics — blocks 34–42.** ⚠ *Cornell figures corrected. See fact-check §3.*

> People on these drugs eat twenty to thirty percent fewer calories a day. Two
> thirds cut back on sugary drinks and alcohol.
>
> Cornell researchers tracked a hundred and fifty thousand American households,
> and found grocery spending falling five point three percent within six months.
>
> Not evenly, though. The deepest cut landed exactly where you'd expect. Savory
> snacks fell ten point one percent. The chip aisle took it.
>
> Spending at fast food chains and coffee shops dropped too. More than half of
> users said they were dining out significantly less.
>
> Now scale that up. About one in every eight American adults is currently taking
> one of these drugs. Right now.
>
> Around thirty million have tried them. Households with a G L P one user already
> make up roughly a quarter of the country.
>
> And by twenty thirty, Circana projects those households will account for
> thirty-five percent of every food and beverage unit sold.
>
> J P Morgan ran the numbers on what that does to the food business. Their
> estimate: thirty to fifty-five billion dollars.
>
> In annual sales. Gone. By twenty thirty. From an industry that had never once
> had to plan for customers wanting less.

**Visual.** Stacked bar of US food and beverage sales, GLP-1 household share
growing from a sliver to over a third across 2021 → 2030. Then a **single coral
block detaching from the top of the bar and falling out of frame** on block 42.
That falling block is the money shot of Act Two — it is the first time coral
appears at full size, and the reason coral has been reserved.

## ACT THREE — THE COUNTERATTACK — 7:10–9:50 · blocks 44–59

**Nestlé — blocks 44–47.**

> In May twenty twenty-four, Nestlé launched a brand called Vital Pursuit. Frozen
> bowls, sandwich melts, pizzas on cauliflower crust.
>
> High protein, good source of fiber, and in the company's own words, portion
> aligned to a weight loss medication user's appetite.
>
> It was the first major new brand Nestlé had launched in the United States in
> nearly thirty years. Fourteen frozen meals.
>
> They built an entire product line for people who had stopped being hungry. That
> isn't a defensive move. That's a land grab.

*"Portion-aligned to a weight loss medication user's appetite"* is Nestlé's own
wording, from their own release. Block 45's "in the company's own words" is doing
real attributive work — keep it.

**Visual.** Freezer door opening. Products as **illustration, never photography** —
this keeps the video legally clean and stylistically consistent. Callout labels
render as abstract highlight bars in the accent colour, not lettering.

**Conagra — blocks 48–52.** ⚠ *Regulatory line corrected. See fact-check §5.*

> Conagra went a different route. It put a G L P one friendly badge on twenty-six
> Healthy Choice meals. First major brand to do it.
>
> Read the fine print, though. Conagra confirmed that no changes at all were made
> to the meals themselves.
>
> The badge simply flagged products that already existed. High protein, low
> calorie, decent fiber. Same food. New sticker.
>
> U S D A reviewed the labels. But there is still no federal definition of what
> G L P one friendly actually means.
>
> So it's a marketing phrase, not a certification. And it turned out to be an
> extremely effective piece of packaging.

**The pricing move — blocks 55–59.** The most elegant thing in the story, and the
part nobody else in the explainer lane has told.

> But the most elegant move in this entire story isn't a product at all. It's a
> decision about how things get priced.
>
> For decades, package sizes went one direction. Six-packs became twelve-packs
> became twenty-four packs, because volume was the whole business.
>
> Now that customers want less food, the industry is shrinking the packs again.
> But smaller packs carry a higher price per unit.
>
> So a company can sell you meaningfully less food and keep its revenue very
> nearly intact. Fewer calories. Same dollars.
>
> Which means the industry never actually lost the appetite economy. It just
> quietly repriced it. And almost nobody noticed.

**Visual.** 24-pack → 12-pack → 6-pack, shrinking, with a price-per-unit indicator
climbing the whole way (rendered as a rising bar, not digits). Final frame holds
both quantities on screen simultaneously — the shrinking stack and the rising bar
in the same composition. That single frame is the argument.

## ACT FOUR — WHO ACTUALLY GETS TO STOP BEING HUNGRY — 10:00–12:30 · blocks 61–75

⚠ **This act was rewritten.** The original brief's premise — that Medicare and
Medicaid don't cover these drugs — stopped being true on July 1, 2026, 27 days
before this script was fact-checked. See fact-check §1. The act's argument is
unchanged and slightly stronger.

**Discontinuation — blocks 61–67.**

> Here's the part that doesn't make the earnings calls. Roughly half the people
> who start these drugs stop within a year.
>
> A Danish study followed the first seventy-seven thousand people in the country
> to take it. Fifty-two percent quit within twelve months.
>
> Cost. Side effects. Insurance denials. And the drop-off was sharpest among
> younger adults, and among lower income adults.
>
> A review of thirty-seven studies covering more than nine thousand people found
> that after stopping, the average person returns to their starting weight.
>
> It takes about one point seven years on average. The cardiometabolic
> improvements reverse as well, in roughly one point four.
>
> Real world data is gentler, mostly because a lot of people restart, or switch
> to something else. But the direction holds.
>
> For most people, the effect lasts exactly as long as the prescription does.
> Which makes access the entire ballgame.

**Coverage — blocks 68–72.** ⚠ *New material.*

> And in twenty twenty-six, access changed. On July first, Medicare began
> covering these drugs for weight loss for the first time ever.
>
> Fifty dollars a month for eligible Part D enrollees. The federal government
> covers two hundred forty-five. States can buy in at that price.
>
> That is genuinely enormous. It is also, on paper, temporary. The program is a
> demonstration, scheduled to end in December twenty twenty-seven.
>
> Outside it, the manufacturers sell direct. The lowest dose runs about a hundred
> and forty-nine dollars a month through the government's discount site.
>
> But maintenance doses are the real number. Two hundred ninety-nine to four
> hundred forty-nine on Lilly's platform. A similar range from Novo.

**Visual.** The through-line plate's Act Four beat: the small freezer-tray plate
sits behind a paper barrier. On block 68 the barrier **lifts partway**. On block 70
the camera pushes in and reveals the barrier is **hinged** — it can come back down.
That hinge is the whole act in one image, and it replaces the original brief's
"gate stays shut" staging.

Block 69 carries the **coverage map**, composed to deliberately echo the cold
open's prescription-density map so the video closes its own loop.

**The split — blocks 73–75.**

> A perfectly reasonable price if you have it. An impossible one if you don't.
> And you can see that split in the grocery data.
>
> Overall, households cut food spending five point three percent after starting.
> Higher income households cut theirs eight point two.
>
> The drug works on everybody's biology the same way. It does not work on
> everybody's budget the same way.

**Visual.** Split-screen — two identical grocery carts, two identical kitchens, one
recurring invoice appearing over the left. Then the two-bar comparison. The bar
pair is 5.3 vs 8.2; the original brief's 8.6/4.2 pair could not be verified and was
cut, so **do not animate a below-the-line bar** — there isn't one in the data.

## ACT FIVE — THE UNCOMFORTABLE PART — 12:40–13:30 · blocks 77–81

> For fifty years, the story America told about body weight was a story about
> willpower. Personal responsibility. Discipline.
>
> Then a drug arrived that quiets one signal in the brain, and tens of millions of
> people who had failed every diet stopped overeating.
>
> If a molecule can undo that environment this easily, then the environment was
> doing far more of the work than we admitted.
>
> And the industry's response wasn't to make the food less engineered. It was to
> build a second, smaller, higher-margin line beside it.
>
> **Same store. One aisle built to make you overeat. One freezer case for the
> people who paid to escape it. Only one needs a prescription.**

**Visual.** Final pullback — the two aisles in the same cutout style, connected by a
single unbroken line of shelving. The through-line plate pays off: both plates,
the grown one and the small tray, sit at either end of that line, and the marker
circle from block 6 redraws itself around the empty one. Camera pulls back until
the store becomes a floor plan, then a mark, then black.

Block 81 runs to the 24-word ceiling on purpose. If the take lands slightly long,
let the assembler speed-compress it — a rushed kicker beats a late one, and the
line has to land inside the frame.
